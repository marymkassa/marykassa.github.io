About me

Organized at work, curious everywhere else

I came to operations through mental health work, and I've stayed because I like making complicated things run smoothly.

I graduated from Towson University with a B.S. in Criminal Justice in 2023 and joined EveryMind, a Maryland nonprofit that provides school-based mental health services, that August. My job was administrative support for one program. By my third year I was coordinating operations for four programs and grants, and I'd built the systems behind a new program from scratch.

The work taught me that good operations are mostly invisible. When budgets are tracked, orders arrive on time and new staff can get into their schools on day one, clinicians can focus on students.

Next, I want to move into program and project management roles where I can manage larger projects and have more say in how programs are funded and run. I'm completing the Certified Associate in Project Management (CAPM) certification in January 2027.

Program launch · EveryMind · 2024–present

Launching Project Wellness

A new state-funded program needed to run in 7 schools with no systems in place. I built the budget, purchasing, onboarding and reporting systems it runs on today.

7schools launched
12frontline staff onboarded
~$75Koperating budget I split across schools in year one
2+ yrssystems still in use

Context

In 2024, EveryMind launched Project Wellness to bring mental health services into 7 Prince George's County public schools. It's funded by a state grant from the Maryland Community Health Resources Commission. On-site work began in September 2024, once the agreement with the school district was in place. Each school has a therapist, a therapeutic mentor and a family care manager, and most staff split their time between two schools.

The program had funding and a staffing plan, but no way to track budgets, buy supplies, onboard staff or report results. Leadership asked me to build those systems, starting from the ones I supported for our established Montgomery County programs.

My role

I was the program's administrative and operations coordinator, working with the program director, division director and finance team. I didn't supervise staff, and leadership set the budget totals. Here's how my responsibilities broke down:

I owned

  • Site shortlist analysis
  • Year-one budget split by school
  • Budget and purchasing trackers
  • Onboarding and credentials
  • Monthly staff reporting

I coordinated

  • Proposal and service definitions
  • Budget change requests
  • School district accounts

I supported

  • Relationships with school partners
  • Groups and events on site

What I did

  1. Built the site shortlist

    I ranked 13 candidate schools using demographic and need data, including free and reduced-price meal rates. Leadership chose the final 7 from my list.

  2. Split the first-year budget across schools

    Leadership set each line item. In the program's first year, I decided how much each school received, wrote down my reasoning, and moved money mid-year from slower-spending schools to busier ones.

  3. Designed the tracking system

    I built Excel trackers that show the remaining balance for every budget line and school, plus an expense workbook that codes each charge by program and school and tracks it until it posts.

  4. Set up a monthly purchasing cycle

    I created request forms, an approval matrix and a fixed monthly calendar so 15 staff across 7 schools knew exactly when and how to order.

  5. Standardized onboarding

    Every new hire meets with me on their second day. I handle CPR certification (now completed before their start date) and coordinate the district accounts staff need to work inside schools.

  6. Shaped the evaluation plan

    I worked with an outside evaluation firm on every section of the program's evaluation plan: its logic model, evaluation questions and the metrics each role reports. Leadership then approved it for the funder.

  7. Rebuilt the client tracker

    I redesigned the tracker staff use to log clients, with automatic duplicate checks, service tiers and a metrics report that calculates itself. Closing the numbers for each report went from about two months to about two weeks.

  8. Built the monthly staff reports

    I created separate monthly report templates for each role so staff record their work the way the funder measures it.

The monthly purchasing cycle

FridayStaff submit requests
MondayManager or supervisor approves
TuesdayI order and log final costs
Thursday–FridayDeliveries reach schools

Repeated every month from August through May. Each purchase type has a set approver.

Challenges

  • Launch timing. The contract started in July, but schools didn't open until September and hiring ran behind. I onboarded each hire as they arrived so nobody started without access.
  • New partners. Schools weren't sure what the program was there to do. Leadership worked with principals, and I helped staff run groups and school events so the program could show its value.
  • Measuring the work. There was no established way to count services, and the same student could be logged more than once. I added duplicate checks to the client tracker and kept revising the staff reports until they matched what the funder needed to see.

Results

  • All 7 schools are running with a full frontline team.
  • Report close time dropped from about two months to about two weeks after I rebuilt the client tracker.
  • Every system I built in 2024 is still in use, updated as requirements changed.
  • My spending analysis supported a change to the state contract budget that moved money into school supplies, including monthly water service for 3 schools.
  • When a second grant came through under the same state funding, I copied the full setup to launch it.

What I learned

  • Plan around when a program can actually start, not just the contract start date. In schools, that's the academic calendar.
  • Budget requests hold up better when they're based on actual monthly spending. We used that approach for year two.
  • A system pays off twice when you design it so it can be copied.

Volunteer research · TecNurtur · November 2026

Best free tools for running a small nonprofit

Small nonprofits run on tight budgets, so I researched which software they can use at no cost and recommended a 4-tool setup that covers all of their core operations for $0.

20tools compared
8operational areas covered
4tools in the recommended stack
$0software cost for the stack

Context

TecNurtur is a youth-led nonprofit that teaches technology courses and workshops to seniors, children and community members, partnering with local food banks to reach underserved communities. I volunteer on research projects that small organizations can put to use.

Plenty of software advertises a free plan, but the limits are easy to miss: user caps, storage limits, watermarks, eligibility rules, or the one feature you need sitting behind a paid upgrade. This project set out to make those limits visible in one place.

Goals

  • Compare at least 20 software tools.
  • Group them into 8 operational areas.
  • Show each tool's free-plan limits side by side.
  • Recommend a short software stack a small nonprofit could adopt.

How I approached it

  1. Mapped the work a nonprofit actually does

    I sorted day-to-day operations into 8 areas: accounting, data collection, document management, events and fundraising, project management, scheduling, design and marketing, and communications.

  2. Set the evaluation criteria

    For every tool I checked the same limits, so the comparison was fair:

    User capsStorageBranding and watermarksCredit card requiredNonprofit eligibilityFeatures behind a paywall

  3. Researched 20 tools

    I used vendor documentation and nonprofit technology sources such as TechSoup, recording what each tool does and where its free plan stops.

  4. Built a recommended stack

    I looked for the fewest tools that would cover every area without a paid upgrade, and noted that free-plan terms change often and should be confirmed before adopting.

The recommendation: 4 tools, 8 areas

Microsoft 365 for nonprofits covers five of the eight areas on its own. Three specialized tools fill the gaps.

Document managementMicrosoft 365
Data collectionMicrosoft 365
Project managementMicrosoft 365
Productivity and schedulingMicrosoft 365
CommunicationsMicrosoft 365
Design and marketingCanva
Accounting and financeWave
Events and fundraisingEventbrite
Microsoft 365CanvaWaveEventbrite

What I learned

  • "Free" needs a definition. Comparing on fixed criteria showed which plans are truly usable and which are trials in disguise.
  • Fewer tools is usually better for a small team. One platform covering five areas means fewer logins, less training and one place for files.
  • Recommendations should come with an expiration note. Free-plan terms change, so the report tells readers to confirm terms before adopting.

Financial operations · EveryMind · 2023–present

Multi-program budgets and reconciliation

I help program directors split operating budgets across schools, track what every school spends, and make sure every charge matches finance's records. Since 2023 I've processed and reconciled more than $675,000 in spending.

$675K+processed and reconciled since 2023

Context

Each program receives its operating money as one pot per budget line: supplies, client activities, therapeutic supplies and so on. Leadership sets those totals, and each program director decides how much each school gets, with my tracking data and input. My job is to track what every school spends and prove that every charge was right. I do that for three programs.

Leading one program's allocation

Project Wellness's first year was the one time I made the allocation decisions myself. In that year, both the program and its director were new. Leadership asked me to work with the two managers to work out which schools needed more money and to forecast spending for the rest of the year.

  1. Checked spending to date

    I pulled what each school had spent so far from my site trackers.

  2. Gathered each team's plans

    The managers asked their school teams what they expected to spend for the rest of the year.

  3. Compared plans to actual patterns

    I checked those plans against each school's monthly spending history in my trackers.

  4. Decided and explained the moves

    I moved money from schools held back by low caseloads, space problems or slow referrals to schools that were running ahead, and wrote down my reasoning for the program director and division director, who approved it.

Result: every school-level dollar was spent by year end, on schools that could use it.

The monthly ledger check

Every month finance sends a ledger listing each charge's vendor, amount, date, school and program. I review it with each manager for their schools.

1. Ledger arrivesFinance sends it monthly, and the director shares it with me and the managers
2. CompareI match it against my trackers with each manager, school by school
3a. Totals matchThe month is closed
3b. Something's offI check Concur, managers check staff check requests, and I send finance the correction with everyone copied

Keeping every charge audit-ready

Program credit card reports go to finance every two weeks, so problems have to be fixed fast. Before each report, I make sure that:

  • Every charge has a receipt. If one is missing, I track it down.
  • Receipts are itemized. If the store's receipt isn't, I get one that is.
  • No sales tax was paid. The organization is tax-exempt, so I get any tax refunded.
  • Every charge carries its accounting code and a written reason. A report can't be submitted without them.

What I learned

  • A budget is a forecast, not a promise. Checking it against real spending every month is what keeps money from going unused.
  • Fixing small problems every two weeks is much easier than fixing a year of them during an audit.
  • Managers trust the numbers more when they review them with me instead of receiving them.
